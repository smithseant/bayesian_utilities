# Space-Filling Designs

## Motivation

The background, here, is an interesting story because pursuing space-filling designs was never an end in itself.  In an environment where complex and expensive computer simulations are needed to answer important questions and where input uncertainty is must be propagated to the output of those simulations, surrogate modeling and emulation is a powerful approach to significantly reduce computational workload.  However, the selection of which cases to run (the design) is an area that can have a significant effect on the accuracy of the emulation — reducing the required number of simulations for a fixed accuracy or increasing the accuracy for a fixed number of simulations.  With the desire to make a design selection that is beneficial in this way, there is a sequence of increasingly precise mathematical statements for such an optimal design:
- **1)** An intuitive approach would be to start with a few points and build an emulator, then find the point in parameter space with the largest emulator uncertainty and choose to run the next simulation there.  Considering Gaussian Processes as a common emulation technique, if the kernel parameters were known up front then the prediction for error is independent from the simulation outputs and the properties of the emulation process can be used (without fully generating the emulator) to build up an optimal design.  Hopefully it is clear that this approach could be extended for prior distributions on the kernel parameters.  It also applies, with different objective functions to other emulation techniques.
- **2)** That design process can be improved by recognizing that instead of selecting based on the largest error before adding a new point, it would be more effective to minimize the largest error that would result after adding the new point.  Sure, when adding points one by one it is quite intuitive that adding the next point where uncertainty is currently largest will provide the best benefit — however, the resulting uncertainty is what we actually care about, and this is a more mathematically precise description of that goal.  Another twist offered by this approach is that it allows for two or even many points to be added in batches — rather than adding sequentially one at a time.  The whole design procedure, actually, can be seen as a single multi-dimensional optimization.  While this is a more mathematically complex optimization, it does offer potential for a better final design.
- **3)** It can be argued that this line of reasoning would be further improved by shifting away from the objective of minimizing the largest resulting uncertainty to minimizing the total resulting information entropy.  This would factor in the possibility that moderate uncertainties over a large region would be considered more detrimental than a large uncertainty in a very small region.  On the other hand, this added sophistication does come with some further mathematical complexity — an integral over the high-dimensional parameter space is required to evaluate the objective function.

But notice how this line of thinking gets intense quickly.  Moreover, there are alternative factors that could potentially need to be factored in: what if coverage of the simulation output space becomes important (active learning), what if the emulator is used in a likelihood to compare to experimental data (Bayesian updating of the posterior), what about multi-fidelity modeling for the emulation, what if the simulation provided gradient outputs, etc.  All of this is to say, there is also strong motivation to keep this process as simple and flexible for modification as possible.

Fortunately there is a principle from statistical design of experiments that applies well here.  Experience shows that a design determined using one specialized approach will provide much of the benefit for most similar situations as a design determined using a related approach.  Yes, one can almost always construct particular scenarios to demonstrate the advantages in the differences.  But in the real world those constructed scenarios usually represent extreme corner cases.  Stated more concretely, consider:  in most cases is a design constructed using approach **3** in that list going to be significantly different than a design from approach **2** — is the benefit significant in any way?  By the same token, is a design from approach **1** going to be much different?  Let's go the next step: looking at approach **1** in the context of GPs, the emulator uncertainty is only a function of distances between points.  In this regard, wouldn't any design that provides reasonable space-filling properties give a similar result and provide much of the benefit of any of these other approaches?  The simplicity of space-filling designs is undeniably compelling.  In the end, the motivation to pursue space-filling designs, therefore, is not to directly compute a universal optimum for any particular criterion — but that they provide a simple, flexible, and robust approximation across a surprisingly broad range of objectives.  They can even provide a reasonable initial guess when a more specialized approach is needed.


## Overview of methods

The space-filling methods are subdivided into **generative** designs (for uniform distributions on the unit box) and **sub-sampling** designs (for arbitrary distributions represented by a large number of samples).

- **Generative:**  `lhs_design`, `oa_lhs_design`, `sobol_design`, `optimal_of_designs`
- **Sub-sampling:**  `greedy_maximin_design`, `support_points_design`


## Observations & recommendations

The space-filling methods are subdivided into generative-design methods and sub-sampling design methods.  The generative space-filling design methods are intended for use in the context of a standard-uniform distribution.  They can often be generalized to more complicated distributions by variable transformation(s) when such transformations are known and depending on which space-filling properties need to be preserved.  The generative space-filling methods included, here, center on Latin-hypercube sampling and Sobol sampling.  The properties and behavior of these approaches are quite well known, so no further recommendations are given.  The sub-sampling space-filling design methods are intended for use in the context of more complex distributions.  The mentality is that a complex distribution can generally be represented by a large number of proposal points (most commonly sampled using MCMC) and the space-filling design procedure sub-samples from that set.  The sub-sampling space-filling methods included, here, are greedy maximin and support points.  Since these methods are less well known, the following observations and recommendations are provided for guidance.

There are five principal factors that affect the behavior of greedy maximin and support points subsampling:
- number of active dimensions;
- number of proposal/candidate points — this is often dictated by the expense of a single simulation and total computational budget;
- number of points retained in the final space-filling design;
- the tempering parameter $\beta$ (where $\beta \rightarrow 0$ results in flatter selected distribtution, and $\beta \rightarrow 1$ results in a selection that is representative of the original proposal distribution).

Recommendations for each method are based on these factors.  They are also limited to qualitative observations of these routines in various situations, although most are consistent with more quantitative investigations.
- If the number of proposal points is greater than some soft threshold ($\sim \! 40,000$ on my machine), then the support-points algorithm begins to bog down since it requires all pairwise distances between points.  This doesn't necessarily need to be a deal breaker (except in cases of extremely large dimensionality), since one can randomly sample down to a reasonable number.
- Both greedy maximin and support points exhibit poor behavior for small final number of design points relative to the number of active dimension.  *There is a loose analogy with finite differencing — where you need at least a certain number of points to resolve the basic behavior before getting into the convergent regime.*  A closer analogy is a heuristic learned from David Higdon and supported by Jerry Sacks for GPs:  $10 \times$ the number of active dimensions.  This heuristic should not be taken too seriously, because of course response surfaces with shorter correlation-lengthscale (higher-wave-number) behavior over a larger domain will require more training samples.  But the voice of experience indicates, "it is surprising how well it works as a starting point."  
  When considering only the qualitative ability of a space-filling sub-sampling to represent a distribution (with emphasis on its tails), the functional response it irrelevant.  So, the heuristic may be even more applicable to that metric.  Ultimately, on the other hand, the required design size should be based on the accuracy of the resulting emulator.
- If the samples do not have corresponding probability information or the probability is uniform over its support, then tempering can provide no benefit.  The downside of using greedy maximin, in these situations, is that it always selects the points farthest into the tails first — in high dimensions with few space-filling points they lie almost exclusively on the outer shell.  Furthermore, their extremity depends on the proposal sampling size — larger sets are more likely to include points farther into the tails.  The downside of using support points, in these situations, is the obvious problem of underrepresenting the tails.  **As a result it is recommended, in the situation of no probability information or a uniform proposal distribution, to** take a two step approach: first, down-select the proposal set by random sampling from the proposals; then, for coverage of the tails use greedy maximin on the reduced set.  (The down-select number provides stochastic control of the distance into the tails and by extension controls the outer shell area an the number of design points necessary to fill it.)  *The remaining recommendations apply when probability information is provided and the distribution is mostly non-uniform.*
- Each method performs somewhat better on its native end of the tempering spectrum.  Greedy maximin does more poorly for $\beta \gtrsim 2/3$, while support points struggles at the other extreme, $\beta \lesssim 0.1$.  To emphasize, these values represent very soft guidelines rather than hard thresholds.
- As mentioned, both methods deteriorate with small number of final design points — but greedy-maximin tends to break down first.  One specific observation is that (in addition to the outer-shell behavior), the greedy maximin method tends to under sample the concave side of sharply curved density contours and over sample the convex side.  This effect is a natural consequence of optimizing for neighbor distance when too few points are available to resolve the geometry and fill the volume of the distribution.  For this reason, in the sparse regime for number of points in the final design it is recommended to use the support-points method.
- When the number of design points is large relative to the number of active dimensions, greedy-maximin sub-selection subsampling appears to provides better qualitative behavior and is recommended as a starting point in that regime.
